# Grader notes: Longest Increasing Path in a Matrix

One deviation from the classic version: the grid is at most 50 by 50 (the classic allows 200 by 200), so that the longest possible climb, 2,500 cells, fits a memoised depth-first search on the test thread's default stack. Heights keep the classic range, 0 to 2^31 − 1. The answer is a length, so no tie rule is needed even when several climbs are longest.

Recursion depth: the reference, the peel, is iterative. The walkthrough's Better, the memoised search, recurses as deep as the climb it follows, at most 2,500 here. A version of it that tries the row neighbours first reaches the full 2,500 on the rising snake test, and it passes every test on the kit's test thread with the JIT off (`-Xint`). Probes of the walkthrough's memoised search on a default test thread, on walled corridors 200 cells wide, fitted 4,623 cells and overflowed at 5,226 with `-Xint`, fitted 6,633 and overflowed at 7,236 on a cold JVM, and fitted 17,286 and overflowed at 18,291 once compiled; the walkthrough quotes these as the point where the recursion stops fitting, and a 200 by 200 grid can hold a climb of about 20,000 cells. The naive solution, the brute force, recurses at most 2,500 deep as well.

Tests cover: the sample (9 on the 3 by 4 grid, 6 on the 2 by 5 grid, 1 on the level 3 by 2 grid); a 2 by 5 grid where stepping to the lowest or to the highest higher neighbour finds 4 and starting from the lowest cell finds 5, against 6; a staircase of 1 to 4 along a diagonal, all side neighbours 9 (2, where diagonal steps would give 5); level ground (`[[1, 2, 2, 3]]` 2, and a 2 by 3 grid that a climb over equal heights would cross in six cells, 3); a single row and a single column; heights 0 and 2,147,483,645 to 2,147,483,647 in one grid; 300 seeded random grids of up to 5 by 5 (no 1 by 1 or 3 by 3 grids) with heights 0 to 6, checked against a search in the test over every rising walk; and a winding corridor of 1,274 rising cells through a 50 by 50 grid of 2,147,483,647, numbered from either end, where the answer is 1,275 because the climb ends with a step up into the wall. A 1 by 1 grid is not tested: every one is a single value away from a well-known sample.

The three timed tests are 50 by 50: a snake that rises by 1 along each row and turns down into the next, 0 to 2,499 (2,500, every cell), the same snake falling from the top-left corner, and a pyramid whose peak is 100 at row 25, column 24, falling by 1 per step away from it (51, from the corner at row 0, column 49). In all three the rising walks branch at nearly every cell.

Traps, each checked with a scratch solution: stepping to the lowest higher neighbour fails the sample (8), the greedy test (4) and the random test; stepping to the highest fails 8 tests, among them the sample (4), the greedy test, the single row, the range test, the corridor and both snakes; allowing diagonal steps fails 4 (the sample's second grid gives 7, the greedy test, the staircase and the random test); stepping onto equal heights in the memoised search recurses forever and overflows the stack on 6 tests, and a backtracking search over equal heights fails 6 and times out on the 3 timed tests; starting only from the lowest cells fails 4 (the sample gives 2, the greedy test, the single row and the random test).

The timed tests have a limit of 200 ms each (3x is 600 ms). The reference took 1 to 3 ms on the rising snake, 1 to 9 ms on the falling snake and 1 to 6 ms on the pyramid over cold runs. The naive solution, the walkthrough's brute force without a memo, is still running after 4,000 ms (20x) on all three. The walkthrough's Better, the memoised search, is O(m × n) like the reference and passes every test, also with `-Xint`.
