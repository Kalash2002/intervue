# Grader notes: Detect Squares

Deviations from the classic version: the methods take two `int`s, `add(int x, int y)` and `count(int x, int y)`, instead of an `int[]` point; `count` returns a `long`, because copies multiply past an `int` (three corners added 2,000 times each give 8 · 10^9 ways); and a finder can get up to 2 · 10^5 calls (the classic allows 3,000), so that a `count` that looks at every stored point is too slow, which the statement says. Coordinates run from 0 to 1000 as in the classic. The statement says that copies count separately, that `count` stores nothing, and that only squares with sides along the axes and a side longer than 0 count; the tests check each. The tests drive the class through calls on new finders, and each `count` has a single correct answer.

Tests cover: the sample (two squares of side 4 on either side of the query, and a query whose column holds only a copy of itself; a square whose corners were added twice each, 2 x 2 x 1 = 4; a count on an empty finder and a tilted square, both 0); squares in all four directions around (500, 500), one more of side 20, and counts from two of the corners; copies that multiply (6, 10 and 15 ways), and copies of the query point that change nothing; copies of the query point alone, a square missing one corner, the missing corner then added, a count from a corner with four copies, and a point that was only counted and must not be stored; a 30 x 20 rectangle and a square standing on its corner, which do not count, and the rectangle's points made into a real square; the corners and edges of the grid, and squares that would reach past it; counts of 8 and 6 billion; two finders used side by side, which must not share points; 300 seeded random finders of up to 60 calls in 9 x 9 windows at the edges, the corners and the middle of the grid, checked against a count in the test over every side length and direction; and the timed test below.

The traps the walkthrough, the statement and the hints name, each run as a scratch variant: an `int` product of the copies fails the large counts; looking for squares only to the right of the query fails 8 of 10; not skipping a side of 0, which counts copies of the query point, fails 10; looking up columns past the edge of the grid without a bounds check fails 4 (the edges, the large counts, the random finders and the timed test); counting each distinct point once instead of each copy fails 5; and storing the query point in `count` fails 8.

Timing: `manyPointsAndManyCounts` has a limit of 1,000 ms (3x is 3,000 ms). It stores 88,355 different points, dense in the lower-left quarter of the grid (every point with x + 2y divisible by 3) and about ten to a row and a column in the upper-right quarter (every point with x - y divisible by 50), and then makes 50,000 seeded counts in the upper-right quarter, half of them on its diagonal lines so that squares exist. The test's answer walks the query's column arithmetically. The layout lets a solution that walks the query's column or its row, keyed in any reasonable way, pass, while one that looks at every stored point does about 4 · 10^9 steps. The reference took 30 to 41 ms over six cold runs. The naive solution is the walkthrough's Brute force, a list of every copy scanned for each diagonal partner; with the limit raised 10 times it does not finish in 10,000 ms. The walkthrough's Better, a map of copies with every distinct point tried as the far corner, does not finish in 10,000 ms either, and a faster form of it on arrays took 7,853 ms. Column or row walks over other stores pass: nested maps (184 ms), rows instead of columns (145 ms), one map keyed by a `long` (414 ms) and one keyed by strings (450 ms).
