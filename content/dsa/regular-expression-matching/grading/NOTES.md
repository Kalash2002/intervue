# Grader notes: Regular Expression Matching

The pattern language is the classic one, defined exactly in the statement: lowercase letters, `.` for any one letter, and `*` after a letter or a dot for any number of copies of that piece, none included; a star never starts the pattern and never follows another star; the match must cover the whole text. Two things differ from the classic version, both stated in the constraints: the text and the pattern may each be up to 100 characters long, so that the backtracking search can be timed, and either may be empty. Every test pattern is valid under that definition. The answer is `true` or `false`, so no tie rule is needed.

Tests cover: the sample (`toast`/`to.a*st` true, where the dot takes the a and `a*` takes none, `seventeen`/`s.*en` true, `banana`/`ban*` false, the empty text with `x*y*` true); a starred piece matching no letter at the start, in the middle and at the end (`x*lamp`, `la.*mp`, `lampy*`, and `e*ok*xq*` for `ox`); a starred piece that must leave letters for what follows (`pinpoint`/`p.*nt`, `beef`/`be*ef`, `toot`/`to*ot`, all true); a star that repeats only the piece before it (`haha`/`ha*` and `bandana`/`ban*.` false, `tool`/`to*l` true); the whole text (`pinpoints`/`p.*nt`, `stars`/`st.r`, `star`/`stars` and `upstart`/`star`, all false); a dot is one letter (`cart`/`c.t` false, `cot`/`c.t` true, the empty text with `.` false); `.*` mixing letters (`road`/`r.*d`, `kayak`/`k.*k`, true); empty strings (both empty true, `mint` with the empty pattern false, the empty text with `q` false); and 300 seeded random cases, patterns of 3 to 6 pieces over `a`, `b` and `.`, with half the texts written out from the pattern and half random texts of 3 to 10 letters over `ab` (195 of the 300 are true, 45 of them among the random texts), checked against a search in the test over every number of copies for each starred piece. Patterns of one or two pieces and very short random texts are left out of the random test, and the seed was chosen, so that no random case is a classic sample, or one edit away from one, even with its letters renamed.

The three timed tests are false, with texts of 100 letters and patterns of 61 or 62 characters. Each pattern fails at a piece in its middle and fits both ends of the text, so a search from the front and a search from the back both meet a great many ways to place the pieces on either side before they reach the failure:
- `ab` 50 times against `.*b` 10 times, a `c` and `.*b` 10 more times: the text has no c. From the front, the first ten b's can sit on C(50, 10), about 10^10, sets of the text's b's; from the back, the last ten on C(49, 9), about 2 × 10^9, since the last must be the final b.
- `ab` 50 times against `.*b` 10 times, a `b` and `.*b` 10 more times: the pattern needs a b straight after a b, and the text never has two b's together. The ways are the same as in the first test.
- `abb` 33 times and an `a` against `.*a` 10 times, `.a` and `.*a` 10 more times: the pattern needs an a two letters after an a, and the text's a's are three letters apart. From the front, C(34, 10), about 1.3 × 10^8, sets of a's; from the back, C(33, 9), about 3.9 × 10^7.
With the middle piece left out, each pattern matches its text, so the middle piece alone makes each answer false.

Traps, each checked with a scratch solution: a starred piece that must match at least one letter fails the sample (`to.a*st`), the no-letter test and the random test, and times out on the timed tests; a starred piece that takes every letter it can and never gives one back fails the sample, the no-letter test, the leave-letters test, the mixing test and the random test; one that takes as few as it can fails the sample, the leave-letters test and the random test; reading `*` as a wildcard of its own, as in file names, fails the sample, the no-letter test, the repeats-one-piece test and the random test; stopping with `true` once the pattern is used up, whatever is left of the text, fails 5 of 12: the sample, the repeats-one-piece test, the whole-text test, the empty-strings test and the random test; letting `.*` repeat only one letter fails the sample, the leave-letters test, the mixing test and the random test.

The timed tests have a limit of 300 ms each (3x is 900 ms). The reference, two rows of the table, took 6 to 17 ms on the first (it includes the warm-up of the loop), 0 to 11 ms on the second and 0 to 7 ms on the third over cold runs. The naive solution is the walkthrough's brute force, which matches from the front and tries no copy and then one more copy at each starred piece; it is still running after 3,000 ms (10x) on all three. Its recursion is at most 201 deep, which fits the stack. Three other slow versions are still running after 3,000 ms on all three as well: the same brute force written over beginnings of the two strings, which compares last letters first; the brute force that tries one more copy before no copy; and Java's own `String.matches`, which backtracks the same way. The walkthrough's Better, the memoised recursion, is O(n × m) like the reference and passes every test, also interpreted (`-Xint`).
