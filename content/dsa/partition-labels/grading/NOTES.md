# Grader notes: Partition Labels

The string may hold up to 100,000 letters (the classic version allows 500) so that the O(n²) brute force can be timed out; the letters are lowercase, as in the classic. The answer is unique: a cut is allowed exactly when no letter appears on both sides of it, that does not depend on any other cut, so the most pieces come from making every allowed cut, and any other valid cutting has fewer pieces. The tests compare the returned list piece by piece and then by its number of pieces, never by `toString()` alone, and a `null` result fails with its own message.

Tests cover: the sample (Example 1 has a letter, `f`, that is its own last copy inside a longer piece; Example 2 is one piece; Example 3 has no repeats); a letter with no copies left that does not end the piece (`qtrtq` gives 5, `mxxmoo` gives 4, 2; cutting where the current letter's last copy is, without the farthest last position so far, the trap the walkthrough and Level 1 hint name, fails 8 of the 10 tests, both timed ones among them); a new letter that does not start a piece (`gjgw` gives 3, 1 and `jkjlkl` gives 6; cutting before every letter not seen yet, also named, fails 8 of 10); pieces at both ends (`ffgiigjj` gives 2, 4, 2); the first letter coming back last (`rkwjqr`, one piece of 6); one letter and one run (`s` gives 1, the forced single letter, and `eeee` gives 4); every letter once (`zyx...a`, 26 pieces of 1); 300 seeded random strings of 1 to 12 letters from `p` to `t`, checked in the test against every set of cuts, keeping the valid one with the most pieces; and two timed strings of 100,000 letters. The first is two pieces, 60,000 and 40,000 long: a to f and u to z appear twice at the start and never again, tied to the rest of the first piece by a `g`, and g to m cycle on to position 59,999; n to t cycle to the end. The second is thirteen pieces, each two letters taking turns (a and b, c and d, and so on), twelve of 7,692 letters and one of 7,696.

Both timed tests have a limit of 200 ms (3x is 600 ms). The reference, one pass with the farthest last position, takes 8 to 21 ms on the first and 4 to 6 ms on the second on a cold JVM, most of it building the string. The naive solution is the walkthrough's brute force, which collects the letters before each position from scratch; it takes about 4.3 s on each, past the limit and 3x. Its mirror, which decides each cut by collecting the letters after it, times out on both. A brute force that collects only the letters of the current piece and stops at the first shared letter after the cut takes over 3 s on the first string and about 0.5 s on the second. One that asks `indexOf` for each letter of the piece, which the JDK runs with vector instructions, takes 0.6 to 0.7 s on the first string whether it tries the letters from a or from z, because six letters at each end of the alphabet have no copies left for most of the first piece. The walkthrough's Better, merging the letters' spans, is O(n) like the reference and passes.
