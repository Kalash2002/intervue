# Grader notes: Happy Number

Same rule and range as the classic version (`1 <= n <= 2^31 - 1`); only the class and method names are this pack's. The answer is a `boolean`, so there is only one correct output.

Tests cover: the sample (356, happy in six steps; 85, which enters the loop of eight; 1111111, which reaches 7 in one step); 1, the powers of ten up to 10^9 and ten 1s, all happy; the single digits 3 to 9, where only 7 is happy; starts that reach 7 after one step (1112, 2111, 1121000) and starts that reach another single digit (1111, 111, 1210); the longest happy chains, six steps (653, 3056) and seven, the most any `int` needs (78999, 99987, 979890, each giving 356 first); six members of the loop (4 is among the single digits) and starts with long ways into it, such as 692, eleven steps; the largest `int`s (2147483647 and 2147483646 are not happy, 2147483645 is the largest happy `int`, and 1999999999 gives 730, the largest value after one step); every start from 1 to 100,000 and the top 100,000 `int`s, each also counted against totals worked out independently (14,377 and 13,386 happy numbers); and 100,000 seeded random starts across the whole range. The test's own answer stops a chain at 1 or at 4, since every unhappy chain passes through 4; that fact was checked for every value up to 730, which covers every chain after its first step.

The traps the walkthrough and hints name, each run as a scratch variant: following the chain until it reaches 1, with no check for a repeat, times out on every test with an unhappy start (8 of 10); stopping at the first single digit and answering whether it is 1 fails the sample, the single digits, the starts that reach 7 and the run of every start up to 100,000; Floyd's walkers started at the same number end before the first round and fail 9 of 10; reading the answer from the slow walker instead of the fast one fails 9; and the Better's table indexed before the first step fails 9, because `n` itself can be far larger than 730. Two more were run: a fixed cap of five steps fails 7 tests and a cap of six fails 4, while a cap of seven is correct for every `int`, since no happy `int` needs more; and a digit loop that drops the leading digit fails all 10. Stopping at the first single digit and answering whether it is 1 or 7, and stopping at 1 or 4, are correct and pass.

No naive solution: time is not graded separately for this question (ADR 0008). After the first step every value is at most 730 and no chain has more than 20 different values, so every correct approach, the set, the table and the two walkers, takes O(log n) time per call and they differ only by small constant factors; there is no slower correct approach a timed test could separate. The tests keep limits only to stop a solution that loops forever: 1 second on the small tests and 2 seconds on the three runs of 100,000 starts, where the reference took 31 to 39 ms over six cold runs.
